# Evidence brief — game mechanics for productive vocabulary learning

## Decision question

Which game mechanics can support vocabulary comprehension, spelling, writing
and speaking for an A2-B1 seventh-grade learner without replacing learning
with superficial clicking?

## Evidence table

| Evidence | Finding | Product implication |
|---|---|---|
| Kaya & Sagnak (2022), systematic review, ages 11-18 | Gamification commonly improved enjoyment, motivation, participation and autonomy across ten studies. | Use a quest, progress and feedback, but do not treat engagement as mastery. |
| Hao, Wang & Ardasheva (2021), meta-analysis, 45 studies / 2,374 learners | Technology-assisted vocabulary learning had a large overall effect; mobile delivery and retention were affected by design and context. | Use short mobile sessions and revisit words across the week. |
| Lin & Lin (2019), systematic review/meta-analysis, 33 studies | Mobile-assisted vocabulary learning showed a large positive effect, moderated by autonomy, duration and setting. | Let the learner choose a daily time and resume safely. |
| Webb, Yanagisawa & Uchihara (2020), meta-analysis | Intentional tasks produced sizeable immediate learning, but delayed form and meaning retention were lower. | Repeat retrieval on later days, not only once. |
| Yanagisawa (2016), retrieval experiment | Productive retrieval was stronger for productive knowledge; receptive retrieval supported receptive knowledge. | Move from multiple choice to typing, sentences and speech. |
| Sailer & Homner (2019), meta-analysis | Gamification had positive cognitive, motivational and behavioural effects, with weaker stability for some non-cognitive outcomes. | Reward demonstrated learning; avoid relying on XP alone. |
| Lei & Reynolds (2022), synthesis of 32 word-card studies | Productive/use knowledge is less often trained; initial concentrated learning followed by spaced retrieval is recommended. | Front-load the word set, then space mixed transfer tasks. |

## Consensus and disagreements

Mobile practice, retrieval and distributed repetition are consistently useful.
Gamification can increase participation, but the evidence does not justify
assuming that points, streaks or leaderboards automatically produce durable
language knowledge. Productive language requires productive retrieval.

## Use

- A seven-day narrative quest with a clear daily endpoint.
- Progress bars, XP and badges tied to mastery thresholds.
- Repeated retrieval with expanding delay.
- Recognition first, then spelling, contextual use, writing and speaking.
- A recovery badge and grace day instead of a punitive broken streak.
- Adaptive hints after an unsuccessful attempt, ending in a worked answer and
  a brief self-explanation.

## Avoid or constrain

- No public leaderboard or social comparison.
- No reward for merely opening a page or clicking through.
- No unlimited guessing without reflection.
- No punitive streak loss.
- No claim that a 20-minute timer proves learning.

## Product rules

Each day contains several short rounds and at least one productive task. A
badge requires a meaningful score or completed production. Missed items return
in later mixed rounds. Speaking recordings remain local. Progress is stored on
the device and can be copied as a summary for the tutor.
